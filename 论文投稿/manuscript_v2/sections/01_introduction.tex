Knowledge distillation (KD) transfers predictive information from a teacher model to a smaller student and has become a standard tool for model compression. Recent work on autoregressive language models has substantially expanded the design space of distillation objectives, including alternative divergences, on-policy training, token-adaptive supervision, and teacher-selection strategies. These developments also make clear that a stronger teacher does not automatically produce a stronger distilled student: teacher capacity, student state, the distillation objective, and the training distribution can all affect the outcome. A practical question therefore precedes the design of a more sophisticated distillation algorithm: \emph{given a student that has already been trained, does adding teacher supervision improve over simply continuing maximum-likelihood training from exactly the same state?}

We study this question through \emph{warm-start distillability}. For a fixed student initialization $S_0$, we branch training into a cross-entropy (CE) continuation and a KD continuation, select checkpoints independently using validation likelihood, and measure the paired difference
\[
    \mathrm{Gain}_{\mathrm{KD}}
    =
    \mathrm{NLL}_{\mathrm{CE}}
    -
    \mathrm{NLL}_{\mathrm{KD}}.
\]
Positive gain therefore means that teacher supervision improves upon what could have been obtained by continuing CE alone, rather than merely improving upon an unrelated or randomly initialized baseline. We use this paired design throughout the main experiments and replicate each confirmatory comparison over three independently trained student initializations.

This evaluation reveals a clear transfer boundary. Under the same token-normalized KD formulation, warm-start KD improves test NLL on Penn Treebank and WikiText-2 by $0.1449$ and $0.1557$ on average, respectively, while degrading TinyStories by $0.1072$. The direction is identical across all three student initializations in each dataset. These results establish that positive and negative transfer are both reproducible outcomes of the same basic distillation recipe. They do not, however, identify why the sign changes: the corpora, teachers, and training states differ across these settings. We therefore use the remainder of the study to test several candidate explanations under more controlled interventions.

We first examine teacher quality. Holding the WikiText-2 teacher architecture, branch composition, and effective fusion weight fixed, we compare two differently trained teacher states. The jointly trained teacher improves all three students, whereas the alternative teacher state harms all three; the paired teacher-state contrast is $0.1833$ NLL on average. This intervention shows that the state and predictive quality of the teacher can strongly modulate downstream transfer. Yet global likelihood does not provide a complete rule. A separate Grassmann-only teacher is worse than the matched student on the same held-out subset for every seed, but still yields positive KD gain for all three students. Thus, teacher quality matters, but a scalar ranking by held-out likelihood is not sufficient to determine whether distillation will help.

We next ask whether complementary or heterogeneous supervision explains the remaining behavior. Within a fixed Transformer--Grassmann teacher checkpoint, fused supervision transfers better than either component source alone. This observation might suggest that architectural heterogeneity supplies uniquely useful knowledge. A homogeneous control rejects that interpretation: a Transformer--Transformer ensemble yields better downstream KD endpoints than the Transformer--Grassmann ensemble for all three matched students. The heterogeneous teacher also exhibits greater branch disagreement, yet this does not translate into better distillation. These controls therefore separate the benefit of ensemble supervision from a stronger claim that architectural heterogeneity, or Grassmann-specific representations, are intrinsically more transferable.

Finally, we test whether inexpensive local diagnostics can predict the long-horizon outcomes. Teacher residual likelihood, student--teacher divergence, gradient magnitude, validation-gradient alignment, and a second-order validation-risk approximation all fail to provide a reliable universal separation of beneficial and harmful conditions in our retrospective controlled set. In particular, the local gradient diagnostics incorrectly reject several teacher conditions that later yield reproducible positive transfer. We treat these results as falsification evidence rather than as a new predictive method: they show that the endpoint behavior observed here is not captured by the simple static or local quantities that we tested.

Taken together, our results support a configuration-dependent view of distillability. In the settings studied here, successful transfer is jointly associated with the teacher state, the warm-start student, and the optimization configuration; no single teacher-quality, diversity, architectural, or local-gradient statistic tested in this work accounts for all outcomes. This conclusion complements recent work that improves autoregressive KD through adaptive teaching, alternative divergences, on-policy training, or teacher selection. Our goal is different: rather than proposing another distillation rule, we construct matched comparisons and controlled counterexamples that clarify when standard teacher supervision provides value beyond continued student training.

Our contributions are threefold. First, we establish reproducible positive and negative warm-start KD outcomes under paired CE-relative evaluation, with three independent student initializations per confirmatory setting. Second, through fixed-composition teacher-state interventions and weaker-teacher counterexamples, we show that teacher quality strongly modulates transfer but is insufficient as a standalone distillability criterion. Third, source, ensemble, and local-diagnostic controls rule out several stronger explanations of the observed gains, including a Grassmann-specific heterogeneous-teacher advantage and reliable prediction from the tested local gradient statistics. Together, these experiments provide a controlled empirical characterization of warm-start distillability in autoregressive language modeling.
